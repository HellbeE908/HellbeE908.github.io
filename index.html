SPECTER: a 3D laser scanner for the price of a laptop

A 3D laser scanner bounces laser light off everything around it and records millions of points that together make an exact 3D copy of a room, a building or a park. Professional scanners cost ₹27–81 lakh, so most buildings in India are still measured with tapes and photos. SPECTER does the same job for ₹41,256.

I built SPECTER with one teammate for the Vishwakarma Awards, and we’re writing a research paper on it.

How it works: a small laser sensor spins in a circle, measuring distances in one flat slice. SPECTER slowly tilts that sensor around a horizontal axis, so the slices add up to a full 3D model of everything around it. A Raspberry Pi 5 collects the measurements and saves them in the file format surveyors use. A separate microcontroller and motor driver run the stepper motor, because it has to stop at precise angles.

Testing taught us two things. Our first motor code lost a tiny bit of angle on every move, adding up to 3.6° of error per motor turn without any warning; we changed it to work out every position from scratch, which keeps the error under 0.11°. We also mounted the sensor slightly off-centre, expecting it to spread the measurements more evenly. It didn’t, but it does keep the motor and electronics from getting in the way of the scan.

In the park scan, flat ground comes out flat to within about 1.6 cm. We’re now checking its measurements against a tape measure and a professional survey instrument.

ARRSY: a crash detector that calls for help on its own

In 2022, India had 4,61,312 road accidents and 1,68,491 deaths, according to the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, and many victims wait too long for help. ARRSY, short for Auto Rescue Response System, is a small device that sits in a car, notices when it crashes or rolls over, and sends its location for help, even if nobody can reach a phone.

Inside are a motion sensor that feels sudden jolts and tilting, a GPS receiver that knows where the car is, and a mobile-network modem that sends the alert. A phone app shows what the sensors are reading and where the car is.

The hardest part was avoiding false alarms. To a single sensor, a pothole or sudden braking can look like a crash, so ARRSY checks several signals together and ignores normal road bumps before deciding.

What happens in a crash

  1. ARRSY turns on with the car and keeps watching for jolts, tilting and sudden stops.
  2. It notices a crash or rollover, or someone presses the SOS button.
  3. A 15-second countdown starts, so a false alarm can be cancelled.
  4. If nobody cancels, it sends an alert with the car’s location to emergency services.

Built with a teammate and selected for the MVP round of Microsoft Imagine Cup 2023. Made with an Arduino Nano ESP32, an MPU6050 motion sensor, GPS, a cellular modem, TinyML, C and C++, and Android.
